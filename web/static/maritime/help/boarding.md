# Boarding

[Back to the handbook](index.md)

Taking a ship instead of sinking her. She is worth a great deal more afloat.

## Getting hold of her

    grapple Marigold

Throws the irons. It needs her within reach and it needs the two of you moving at close to
the same speed — a hull sheering away takes the iron with her.

**Grapples are a count, not a yes.** How many go across depends on three things, and any one
of them can refuse the boarding on its own:

- **How much of the two hulls are alongside.** Meeting bow to bow gives you one point of
  contact and a couple of irons. Lying properly alongside gives you the whole rail.
- **How fast she is sheering.** The faster the relative motion, the fewer stick.
- **How many hands you have.** Each line costs three of them.

    grapples

What is holding, and how many.

    cut grapples

Lets go. **This takes time, and more of it the more lines are fast.** Clearing twelve irons
with two hulls grinding together is an undertaking — which is the price of having lashed
yourself to her thoroughly in the first place.

More lines also hold harder, though not in proportion: twelve hold about twice as hard as
one, not twelve times. Breaking free has to stay possible, because it is what makes being
boarded survivable and worth trying to survive.

## Carrying her deck

The fight is decided by **how many can be brought to bear**, not by how many each ship
carries.

How many of yours can cross at once is the real length of rail in contact — so laying
yourself properly alongside is worth doing, and it is a thing you do with the helm rather
than with a button.

How many of hers can meet them is capped at about twice your boarders. Every soul aboard
may turn out; only the front of them is in the fight, and the rest are waiting for somebody
to fall. **This is what stops boarding being decided by headcount.** Three hundred men do
not beat forty marines by three hundred to forty. They beat them by eighty to forty, and
the marines can win that.

Marines cross first, then seamen, then whoever is left. A captain sends the people he
shipped to fight.

### The four ways it ends

- **Your party is thrown back.** Try again, or cut and run.
- **Her deck is carried.**
- **Neither.** Both sides feed in more. Most exchanges end here, and reinforcing is most of
  what a boarding action actually is.
- **Nobody met you** — she has nobody left standing, and she is taken.

### Why you beat her down first

While she has men to spare she meets whatever crosses with twice its number, however wide
the contact — so frontage changes the size of the fight and not the odds. The moment she
*cannot* field twice what comes over, every extra man across is a man she has nobody to
meet.

That is the argument for grape and for gunnery before you lay yourself alongside. Fill her
with grape, then board what is left.

## Striking

    strike

Hauls your colours down. It is a fact the whole action can see, and it is one of the
conditions of being taken.

Typing it again puts them back up, which is worth knowing: a prize crew can be overwhelmed,
and a ship that has been retaken is not still surrendered. `strike colours` and `surrender`
do the same thing.

## What being taken means

Ownership passes, and command with it. It is deliberately harder than sinking her, and
worth more.
