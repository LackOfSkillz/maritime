Two things can kill her, and they want opposite things from you.

## Fire and water want the same hands

**Fire wants her slowed** so the wind is not fanning it. **Water wants her slowed** too —
but for a different reason, and the moment you are fighting both at once you are choosing
which one kills you.

    > pumps
    She has 22 per cent of her buoyancy in water. She is making water at 1.4 per cent a
    minute. Nobody is on the pumps. At this rate she has about 55 minutes.
    Taking the way off her would slow what is coming in.

    > man pumps 12
    12 hands are on the pumps. That is enough to hold it.

Every hand on the pumps is a hand not at the guns and not on the fire. Nothing here decides
that for you, and nothing warns you about it either — the cost is the point.

## Sinking is a process, not a threshold

She does not have a hit point total that runs out. She makes water, you fight it, and the
question is only ever *how long at this rate*. That number moves the instant you take the way
off her, put more hands on, or get a sail under her bottom:

    > fother
    The hands are getting a sail over the bows and working it aft under her. A quarter of
    an hour, and she will be the better for it.

## Where a hole is matters more than how big

A shot into her bulwark is splinters. The same shot three feet lower is a ship going down —
and the model needs nothing clever to say so:

> **A hole is under water when it is lower than she is deep.**

Her draft already grows with what is in her hold, so a laden ship drowns from a hole a light
one would shrug off. And a ship already making water sits deeper, which puts holes that
*were* clear under the surface — so she settles onto her own wounds. Nobody wrote that
spiral; it falls out of measuring a hole against a waterline that moves.

What comes through one follows the head of water above it, by the square root — so a hole
well under her is bad without being instantly fatal, which is what keeps a holed ship a fight
rather than a cutscene.

**A shot goes into her bow, her waist or her quarter** by the bearing it came over. Which is
both what happens and why crossing an enemy's bow or stern was worth the manoeuvring.

## She fills from the bottom

As the water rises, a compartment becomes untenable at about half its own height — a man
works in water to his knees and not to his chest. Her people are driven **up a deck at a
time**, not drowned all at once at the end.

Up, not off. She has not foundered; the hold has.

## Mending her

    > repairs
    Wrong with her:
      hull            34 per cent gone
      rigging         12 per cent gone
    Her mainmast is jury-rigged and wants a yard.

    > set repairs 8

`repairs` reports her track by track, tells you the rate the carpenter's party is working
at, and names what her own people cannot put right however long they have. Heaving to and
handing her canvas works several times faster than mending under way, and it says so.

Her people mend hull, oars and canvas at sea. A mast they can only jury-rig, and a jury rig
is the reason a ship carrying one has somewhere she needs to be:

    > refit

`refit` needs a yard alongside — *"A yard cannot reach her out here"* is the refusal at sea.
It is the only thing that lifts a jury rig, and it is paid out of her own purse.

## When she goes

Her people take to the boats, and whoever gets no seat goes into the water. **Seats are
scarce**, and boats get shot away during the action — so "did you keep your boats?" is a
tactical question with a consequence that outlives the fight.

What the water then does to somebody is not decided here. That is a statement about how harsh
a game is, and it collides with whatever health the game already has. She puts them there and
says so; the game decides the rest.
