# The guns

[Back to the handbook](index.md)

    guns

The battery: what she carries, which arc each gun bears in, whether it is loaded, and how
long until it is ready.

## Serving them

    load ball
    load chain
    load grape

**What you load is a decision about what you want to happen**, not a damage number:

| Shot | What it does | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ball` | Opens her hull | You want her sunk, or you want her stopped |
| `chain` | Cuts her rigging | You want her unable to run |
| `grape` | Kills her people | You want her boarded |

That is the whole triangle, and it is why [boarding](boarding.md) and
[capture](boarding.md) matter: a ship you have filled with grape is a ship you can take, and
a ship you have holed with ball is a ship at the bottom, and she is worth a great deal more
afloat.

Reloading takes time. A gun that has fired is not a gun you have.

    fire Marigold

Everything that bears, at her. Guns that do not bear do not fire and do not spend their
charge.

## Waiting for the right moment

    hold fire Marigold
    hold fire starboard

Guns run out and held, waiting. They speak the moment the thing they are waiting for bears,
which in a continuous simulation is a genuinely useful order — "hold your fire until she
crosses the bow" means something on a clock.

**The two forms are not the same order.** Holding for a *named ship* is safe and needs her
identified, so it does nothing in fog or at the edge of vision — which is exactly where you
most want your guns ready. Holding on an *arc* works in any weather and fires at whatever
crosses it. Nothing in the game knows what a friend is. An order to fire at anything that
crosses to starboard is already an order to kill your own consort, and you gave it.

A held shot is laid worse than a considered one. You are pulling the lanyard on a bearing
rather than on a solution.

## What decides whether it tells

Range, the state of the sea, and her aspect. A ship bow-on is presenting her width; broadside
she is presenting her length, three or four times as much. That is why ships under fire turn
towards the guns, and why crossing an enemy's course is worth the trouble — from both sides
of the exchange.

Frightened crews still fire. They fire badly, and slowly, which is
[morale](crew.md) doing its work rather than a separate rule.
