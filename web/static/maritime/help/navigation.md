# Navigation

[Back to the handbook](index.md)

**Where you are and where you think you are are two different questions**, and the gap
between them is the whole of navigation.

    position

Latitude and longitude, the course she is steering, and what she is making. This is her
*reckoning* — what the ship believes, worked up from the courses steered and the distances
run.

It is wrong, and it gets wronger. Every hour under way adds the set of a current nobody
measured and the leeway nobody logged. The reckoning does not know about either, because
nobody aboard can see them.

## Putting it right

    fix

Takes a bearing on something you can see and known, and puts the reckoning back where it
belongs. It also tells you the set — how far the water has carried you since the last fix —
which is the only way anybody learns what the stream is doing.

**Fix whenever you can see anything to fix by.** A ship that has not fixed since yesterday
is a ship whose position is a hopeful guess.

## The paper

    chart

What the chart says is under her, and how far to trust it. Charts are wrong in fixed
places — a shoal drawn a cable from where it is, a depth taken at a state of tide nobody
recorded — and the chart knows it is wrong before you do.

    plot Careenage

Lays a course to a mark by way of safe water, rather than a straight line through whatever
is in between.

## Being taken there

    ports
    make for Careenage

`ports` lists the harbours she could be told to make for, and — more usefully — why she
cannot be told to make for the others: too shallow, too far, no water in the approach.

`make for` hands the whole passage to the sailing master: the course, the marks, the
approach, and alongside at the end of it. See [harbours](harbours.md).

    follow
    belay

Hand him the con, and take it back. `belay` is the important one: it is how you stop him
when you can see something he cannot.
