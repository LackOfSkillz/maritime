# Ramming

[Back to the handbook](index.md)

There is no order for this. You ram a ship by steering into her, and the collision resolves
when your stem reaches her.

**It hurts you too.** That is the whole reason it is a decision rather than a free attack.
A hull driven into another hull is being asked to survive the same collision it is
delivering.

## What decides the damage

**The closing speed**, along the line between you — and the energy goes as the *square* of
it. Twice the speed is four times the collision.

Only closure counts. Overhauling a ship making almost your own speed is a bump however fast
the pair of you are crossing the ground; meeting her head-on closes at the sum of both
speeds, and is the worst collision on the board for exactly that reason.

**Which part of her you hit.** A stem is the strongest part of a ship and her side is the
weakest, so a blow on the beam is worth manoeuvring for. Running along her side rather than
into it is not a ram at all — it glances.

**What is on your bow.** A plain stem spreads the blow and much of it comes back into your
own timbers. A beak is structure built forward of the hull to be hit *instead* of the hull:
it drives more into her and takes less back.

**How big you both are.** A frigate running down a ship's boat destroys the boat and barely
marks her own stem. A boat rowed at a frigate cannot open her, however determined the
rowing — the collision can only contain what the lighter of the two brings to it.

## Being rammed

Your guns fire everything that bears in the moment before she arrives. Nobody has to order
it and there is nothing to declare — a gun crew watching a bow come at them fire.

Mostly they miss. She is coming at you end-on, which is the narrowest she will ever look,
and that is precisely why ramming is worth attempting. And every gun that speaks starts its
reload, so you meet whatever follows the collision with an empty battery.

## Running down her oars

Running down the side of a ship under oars shears off the looms she has out. How much of
her side you ran down decides how many. Her oars break against your topsides as they go,
which costs you a little — but she is the one who loses the ability to move.

A ship under sail alone cannot be sheered. There is nothing out there to break.
