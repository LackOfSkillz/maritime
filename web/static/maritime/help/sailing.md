# Sailing

[Back to the handbook](index.md)

**Sailing is a negotiation, not an order.** You set canvas and steer a course; the wind
decides what that is worth. A ship close to the wind makes less than the same ship with it
on the beam, and no order changes that.

    wind

Where the wind is from, how hard it is blowing, and how she lies to it — which is the one
number that matters. "She has it on the beam, her best point of sailing" is the answer you
want; "she is close-hauled" is the answer you get when you are trying to go where the wind
is coming from.

## Points of sailing

| How she lies | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Running | The wind astern. Comfortable, and not her fastest. |
| Broad reach | Over the quarter. Fast. |
| Beam reach | Square on the side. Her best point of sailing. |
| Close reach | Forward of the beam. Still good. |
| Close-hauled | As near the wind as she will lie. Slow, and hard work. |
| In irons | Head to wind. She makes nothing at all and will not answer. |

You cannot sail straight into the wind. To get somewhere upwind you sail close-hauled on
one tack, then the other, and accept the extra distance.

## How much canvas

    sail working

The plans, from least to most:

| Plan | When |
| --- | --- |
| `furled` | Nothing set. At anchor, or under oars, or hove to. |
| `storm` | Weather that would take the sticks out of her. |
| `reefed` | Blowing hard. |
| `battle` | Going into action — see below. |
| `working` | The everyday plan. What you want most of the time. |
| `full` | Everything she has. Fast, and everything is exposed. |

**More canvas is not always more speed.** Over-pressed in a blow she is slower, harder to
steer and heels further; and everything aloft is something chain shot can bring down.

**Battle sail** is the fighting plan: enough to work her, little enough that there is not
much aloft to lose. A ship going into action under a full press is a ship whose rigging is
about to be somebody's target.

## What is actually moving her

    current

The set and drift — which way the water is going and how fast — and the course she is
making good over the ground, which is not the course she is steering. A ship steering east
in a north-going stream is going somewhere north of east, and the difference is the thing
that puts ships on rocks.

    weather

The wind by its force, the state of the sea, and how far you can see. A heavy sea takes her
way off her and makes gunnery harder; poor visibility means the lookout sees less, and so
do the people looking for you.
