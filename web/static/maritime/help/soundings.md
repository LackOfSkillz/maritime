# Soundings and the ground

[Back to the handbook](index.md)

    sound

Water under the keel, and a warning when it is shoaling. The leadsman calls it as he finds
it — "by the mark seven", "a quarter less eight", "by the deep six" — and the calls are
worth learning, because they arrive before the grounding does.

**A ship that grounds without warning is an accident. A ship that grounds after the
leadsman has called diminishing water is a decision.** Only the second one is your fault,
and only the second one is worth playing.

## Getting it wrong

Running aground is not one thing. It depends on what you hit and how fast:

- **Touched.** You felt it, she came off, no harm.
- **Aground.** She is held. Sand and mud will give her back — see below.
- **Holed.** Rock, at speed. Water is coming in and this is now a different afternoon.

Speed is what decides it. The same reef at two knots and at eight is a scrape and a wreck.

## Getting off again

    kedge

Runs an anchor out astern in a boat and hauls her off by main strength. It costs time and
it costs the crew, and it works when there is deep water behind her to be hauled into.

The other way is to wait. A ship held on sand or mud comes off on the tide, and the tide is
a thing you can read rather than hope about. A ship on rock does not come off on the tide —
she comes off in pieces, or not at all.

## Not getting it wrong

The chart is drawn at a datum, and the tide is not the datum. Two metres on the chart at low
water is more than two metres at high, and less at a spring low. `chart` tells you what the
paper says; `sound` tells you what is actually there.

Where the two disagree, the lead is right.
